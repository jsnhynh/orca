Results¶

  • Architecture tests: all 67 pass, and each retires exactly the instructions the simulation retires.

  • CoreMark: 1,032 per second at 200 MHz, 5.16 per MHz, IPC 1.51. The board log prints 1,090 because CoreMark rounds its run time down to whole seconds; the cycle count gives 1,032.

  • Embench-IoT: 2.31 per MHz over all 19 programs, 461 per second at 200 MHz.

  • Branch prediction: 98.9% of conditional branches right on CoreMark, 2.1 mispredictions per thousand instructions.

  • Linux: no-MMU kernel 6.18.7 reaches a BusyBox shell with 256 MiB of RAM (Linux bring-up).

All the board numbers come from the same prebuilt bitstream and images that ship in the kit, run over JTAG with nothing running on the Arm side. Benchmarks are GCC 15.2 at -O3.

Timing and size¶

The full SoC closes at 5.0 ns, but Vivado placement settings move this netlist anywhere from -0.6 to 0.0 ns, so the 200 MHz build has zero margin and depends on the tools. The only on-chip RAM is the L1 caches; everything else lives in the board’s DDR.

Core Comparison¶

I compared ORCA with three of the best known open-source out-of-order RISC-V cores I could build and run under the same conditions: NaxRiscv (SpinalHDL), RSD (SystemVerilog) and BOOM (Chisel), plus Arm’s published Cortex-A9 figure as an extra datapoint. There are surely other open cores, but these are the ones commonly recognized in the open community that can be built as a 2-wide OoO core. Every open core runs the same CoreMark and Embench sources with GCC 15.2 at -O3 and goes through the same out-of-context Vivado flow on the KV260 part, core plus L1 caches.

ORCA arguably performs the best in its 2-wide class, with Nax the closest and the best 1-to-1 comparison. ORCA is 10.0% faster in CoreMark/s and 4.0% faster in Embench/MHz than Nax, but 1.78x larger, most likely from the routing logic and the larger TAGE predictor.

How ORCA compares, in charts¶

ASIC estimate¶

The FPGA is the main target, but I also synthesized the core and L1 caches with Yosys to meet 1 GHz on ASAP7 standard cells before layout, in about 0.085 mm². It’s only an estimate, with no placement or routing and fake RAM timing. The reports are in results/asap7/, and the flow is on the bring-up page.

Performance, Area, & Timing Tuning¶

To stay competitive I went after frequency, sizing the structures from benchmark and clock measurements and adding IPC only off the critical paths. RTL alone got to 175 MHz.

Past 190 MHz, every failing path was a complete loop that couldn’t be cut in half or shrunk enough to offset routing delay. RTL timing changes got from 175 to about 190 MHz, and the last step to 200 MHz was EDA tool luck rather than an RTL problem. Remap optimization, net-delay placement and extra placement margin, plus letting LLMs run a greedy search over build directive combinations, closed 200 MHz after a weeks-long search with each build taking about an hour on a Ryzen 9800X3D. Most builds with good settings closed around 190 MHz.

Size came well over the competition. Cutting it in half wasn’t plausible without a major redesign, so I made one pass that changed no structure sizes and kept the cycle counts: valid-only clears, LUT RAM read by index instead of wide selects, and one read port per RAM field. It took the core plus L1 from 28.6k to 21.2k LUTs, and the full SoC from 36,304 to 29,496, and it still closes 200 MHz.

PULP IP vs Xilinx IP¶

For the SoC I chose PULP IP for the AXI crossbar and UART 16550. Xilinx’s IP saved about 3,000 LUTs with no performance difference, but I couldn’t close 200 MHz in 20 builds because of seed and placement variance, and I did NOT want another week of searching, so PULP stayed. It might also help if I ever do the full ASIC flow.
